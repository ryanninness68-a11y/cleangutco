Why Am I Always Bloated? Here's What the Research Actually Says. (The Answer May Surprise You)

Bloating isn't normal. It's your gut trying to tell you something.

I used to think bloating was just something that happened after you ate too much.

Like it was a portion control problem. Or maybe a gluten thing. Or just one of those things your body does sometimes and you learn to live with it.

A lot of people are living with it. Quietly, uncomfortably, and without a real answer for why it keeps happening.

What I've found digging into the research is that persistent bloating — the kind that's just always there, not just after a big meal — is often a signal. Not a random one. A pretty specific one, once you know what to look for.

And it almost always points back to the gut.

Here's what's actually happening when you bloat.

Your digestive system is home to trillions of microorganisms — bacteria, fungi, viruses — collectively called your gut microbiome. When that community is balanced, everything moves the way it's supposed to. Food gets broken down efficiently. Gas gets managed. Your gut lining stays intact and does its job.

When the balance shifts — when certain bacteria overgrow, or beneficial bacteria get depleted — the whole system starts working less efficiently. Food ferments differently. Gas builds up in places it shouldn't. The gut lining can become more permeable than it should be. And the result is that uncomfortable, persistent bloated feeling that no amount of peppermint tea seems to fix.

A 2021 review published in the International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health described how disruptions in the gut microbiome — what researchers call dysbiosis — can impair the intestinal barrier and trigger systemic inflammation. That's not just a gut problem. When the barrier breaks down, it affects the whole body.

(Source: Di Tommaso et al., 2021)

There's a specific condition that comes up a lot in the bloating research that most people have never heard of.

It's called SIBO — small intestinal bacterial overgrowth. And it's more common than people realize.

The small intestine is supposed to have relatively few bacteria compared to the large intestine. When bacteria overgrow there — from things like proton pump inhibitors, certain medications, gut motility issues, or even a single course of antibiotics that disrupted the balance — the result is fermentation happening in the wrong place. Which means bloating, gas, distension, and digestive discomfort that can feel completely random and unpredictable.

A review in Clinical and Translational Gastroenterology described bloating, gas, and abdominal distension as some of the most common symptoms of SIBO — and noted that it's frequently underdiagnosed because people assume these symptoms are just normal.

(Source: Rao & Bhagatwala, 2019)

They're common. They're not normal.

The antibiotic connection is worth understanding too — especially if your bloating seemed to start or get noticeably worse after a round of antibiotics.

Antibiotics do what they're designed to do — they kill bacteria. The problem is they're not selective. They take out the harmful bacteria they're targeting and a significant portion of the beneficial bacteria you need at the same time.

A 2022 review in the World Journal of Gastroenterology found that antibiotic treatment induces shifts in the bacterial community composition that closely resemble the gut microbiome patterns seen in IBS — and that antibiotic use is associated with a measurably increased risk of developing IBS afterward.

(Source: Mamieva et al., 2022)

If you've noticed your digestion was never quite the same after a course of antibiotics — you're not imagining it.

So what do you actually do about it?

The research on probiotics for bloating and IBS symptoms is mixed — and I want to be honest about that rather than just tell you probiotics fix everything.

A meta-analysis published in Gastroenterology in 2023 reviewed 82 randomized controlled trials involving over 10,000 patients. The findings were nuanced — some strains showed meaningful benefit for global IBS symptoms and abdominal pain, while results for bloating specifically were more variable depending on the strain.

(Source: Goodoory et al., 2023)

The takeaway isn't "don't bother with probiotics." It's "strain matters." Not all probiotics are the same. Bifidobacterium strains showed the most consistent evidence for abdominal pain, while certain combination formulas showed benefit for bloating specifically.

A 2024 review in Nutrients looking specifically at gut dysbiosis and IBS concluded that probiotics and their metabolites show genuine promise in rebalancing the microbiome and reducing symptoms — and that the field is actively building toward more targeted, strain-specific recommendations.

(Source: García Mansilla et al., 2024)

Here's what I keep coming back to when I read this research.

Most people — me included, for a long time — treat bloating like an inconvenience to manage rather than a signal to pay attention to. Take a digestive enzyme. Try cutting out dairy. Buy the expensive sparkling water.

But if persistent bloating is your gut telling you the microbial community inside it is out of balance — the most useful thing you can do isn't manage the symptom. It's address the foundation.

That's what a gut reset is actually about. Not a detox. Not a crash diet. Just giving your gut the conditions it needs to start rebalancing — the right foods, the right fiber, reducing the things that feed dysbiosis, and adding back what's been depleted.

It's not a complicated fix. But it's a different question than the one most people are asking.

This is not medical advice. Persistent digestive symptoms are worth discussing with your doctor — especially if they're severe or have changed recently. I'm a curious non-expert sharing research I found genuinely interesting.
